# Stylistic Examples
## *The Power of Instinct* — Voice and Prose Standards for the Drafting Agent

> **Authority level: HIGH.** This file is the AI's voice training document.
> Every sentence generated for this book should be benchmarked against the
> examples below. When the drafting agent generates a paragraph, it should
> ask: "Does this sound like the examples in the DO column, or the
> DO NOT column?" The DO NOT examples are equally instructive.
>
> **These are not suggestions.** These are production standards.
> Prose that consistently falls into the DO NOT patterns must be
> regenerated. The editor's threshold for acceptable prose is set
> by the DO examples below.

---

# Rule 1: Vary Sentence Length

## The Principle

The voice of this book alternates deliberately between long, architecturally
complex sentences that carry nuance, and short sentences that deliver impact.
The rhythm is: *build → build → land*. The short sentence is the landing.
It arrives when the reader has been primed by the longer sentences to receive
the conclusion with force.

The drafting agent must read its own output aloud (or process it rhythmically)
and flag any passage where three or more consecutive sentences are the same
approximate length. That is a cadence problem and must be corrected.

---

## DO: Correct Sentence Length Variation

> *The body knew before the mind did. This is not a poetic claim. It is a
> measurable biological fact — confirmed in controlled laboratory conditions,
> replicated across multiple research groups, reproducible with equipment
> you can buy online.*
>
> *Participants in Bechara and Damasio's Iowa Gambling Task were selecting
> cards from the winning decks — demonstrating a clear, consistent behavioural
> preference for the low-risk options — a full thirty cards before they could
> articulate any reason for that preference. Their conscious minds had no
> explanation. Their skin conductance records told a different story entirely:
> elevated sweat gland activity in response to the prospect of choosing from
> a bad deck, registering clearly in the data from the experiment's earliest
> trials. The signal was there. It was legible. The mind simply hadn't been
> told yet.*
>
> *Your nervous system is not waiting for your permission.*

**Why this works:**
- The first three sentences are short, declarative, and build cumulative
  force. They establish the claim before the evidence arrives.
- The middle paragraph is one long sentence — deliberately unwound — that
  gives the reader the full complexity of the evidence.
- The final sentence is punchy, direct, and lands the implication.
  The reader absorbs the complexity of the middle paragraph and then
  receives the short landing sentence with visceral force.

---

## DO: A Second Example (Chapter Opening Rhythm)

> *She had seen thousands of fires. She had trained for a decade. She had
> stood in burning buildings in six states, learned their personalities —
> how they breathe, how they spread, how they die. She knew fire the way
> a musician knows their instrument: not through analysis, but through
> a relationship built over years of close, often dangerous attention.*
>
> *And yet, standing in that kitchen, something she could not name told her
> to get her team out immediately.*
>
> *What she was experiencing — what saved four lives that afternoon — was
> not a sixth sense. It was a finely calibrated pattern-recognition system
> doing exactly what fifteen years of experience had trained it to do.
> She was not guessing. She was remembering — in a mode of recall so fast
> that it arrived before language, before thought, before anything she
> could point to and call a reason.*

**Why this works:**
- The first paragraph builds rhythmically with anaphora ("She had... She had...
  She had...") creating a drumbeat of competence before the pivot.
- The pivot — the single short paragraph — is given room to breathe by the
  surrounding longer paragraphs.
- The final paragraph delivers the conceptual payoff at full length,
  rewarding the reader's patience.

---

## DO NOT: Flat Rhythm

> *Phineas Gage was a railroad construction foreman who was working in Vermont
> in 1848. An accident with a tamping iron sent the rod through his head and
> damaged his prefrontal cortex. He survived the accident but his personality
> changed significantly. His colleagues noticed that he had become a different
> person. This case is very important for neuroscience because it showed how
> brain damage can affect decision-making. Antonio Damasio studied cases like
> this and developed the Somatic Marker Hypothesis.*

**Why this fails:**
- Every sentence is approximately the same length and structure.
- The rhythm is monotonous and reads like a Wikipedia summary.
- The words "very important" are explicitly forbidden by `system_rules.md`.
- There is no build, no rhythm, no landing. The reader has no way to know
  which sentence is the important one.

# Rule 2: Use Vivid Analogies

## The Principle

Every complex biological or psychological mechanism in this book must be
translated through a concrete, sensory analogy before the technical
terminology is applied. The analogy creates a mental scaffold. The
terminology then populates that scaffold. Without the scaffold, the
terminology floats without attachment in the reader's working memory.

The analogy comes first. Always. The technical term follows.

**The analogy quality test (from `system_rules.md`):**
- Concrete and sensory (not abstract-to-abstract)
- Proportionate in scale
- Accurate under scrutiny (would not mislead an expert)
- Not clichéd (the banned analogy list is in `system_rules.md` Section 1.3)

---

## DO: Epigenetics Analogy

> *Think of your genome as a complete musical score — every instrument, every
> passage, every note that the composer ever wrote is present in the manuscript.
> Epigenetics is the conductor's annotation system: sticky notes placed over
> certain passages, marked "skip this section for now," or "emphasise this
> phrase strongly." The annotations don't change the underlying notes. The
> notes are permanent. But they determine, powerfully, what the orchestra
> actually plays.*
>
> *What the research on inherited trauma has established is that your parents'
> experience — the famine they survived, the war they fled, the sustained
> threat they navigated — left annotations on the score before you received
> it. You are reading music that has already been marked up. Some of the
> passages playing most insistently in your biology were highlighted by
> someone else's emergency.*

**Why this works:**
- The score analogy is concrete (a physical object), familiar (most readers
  have seen annotated sheet music), and accurate (epigenetic marks do not
  alter the DNA sequence but do alter its expression).
- It does not oversimplify: the analogy correctly conveys that the underlying
  genome is unchanged (the notes are permanent) while the expression is
  modified (the annotations).
- The second paragraph applies the analogy directly to the human experience
  being described, closing the loop from mechanism to meaning.

---

## DO: Default Mode Network Analogy

> *Most companies treat their best thinking as something that happens in
> conference rooms, during working hours, in the presence of a slide deck.
> The neuroscience suggests this is like assuming that a city's most
> creative work happens in its offices rather than on its commutes home —
> in the moments of transition, when the formal agenda has released its hold
> and the mind begins to make connections it could not make while it was busy
> being managed.*
>
> *This is roughly what the Default Mode Network does. It activates most fully
> in the gaps between tasks — when attention is not being directed outward,
> when the prefrontal supervisor has stepped back from its post. In these
> gaps, the brain integrates experience, searches for pattern, runs
> associative connections between domains that deliberate attention keeps
> separate. This is not rest. It is a different kind of work.*

**Why this works:**
- The "city / commute" analogy makes the abstract phenomenon immediately
  visualisable and slightly counterintuitive — which is the hallmark of a
  good popular science analogy.
- The second paragraph applies the technical mechanism with precision
  without abandoning the accessibility established by the analogy.

---

## DO: Somatic Markers Analogy

> *Imagine that every significant decision you have ever made left a physical
> trace — not in your memory, exactly, but in your body's evaluative
> catalogue. A slight muscular tension associated with the type of situation
> that previously led to loss. A warmth in the chest associated with the
> type of relationship that has been safe. Over years, over hundreds of
> decisions, this catalogue builds. It doesn't store the full reasoning
> that preceded each outcome. It stores only the conclusion, encoded as a
> felt sense.*
>
> *This is the Somatic Marker Hypothesis. The body is running a compressed
> archive of your decision history, and when a new situation resembles a
> catalogued category, it retrieves the associated signal and sends it
> forward. The signal arrives before the analysis. It is meant to.*

**Why this works:**
- The opening analogy ("physical trace," "evaluative catalogue") makes
  the mechanism concrete before the technical term arrives.
- The analogy is accurate: somatic markers are precisely the learned
  association between situational categories and bodily states.
- The final sentence ("It is meant to.") uses the short-sentence technique
  from Rule 1 to land the implication.

---

## DO NOT: Abstract Explanation Without Analogy

> *Epigenetics refers to changes in gene expression that do not involve
> alterations to the underlying DNA sequence. These changes are mediated
> by mechanisms such as DNA methylation and histone modification. Research
> has shown that epigenetic changes can be transmitted from parents to
> offspring, which has significant implications for our understanding of
> how trauma is inherited across generations.*

**Why this fails:**
- The reader is given the technical mechanism before they have any
  conceptual scaffold to place it in.
- There is no analogy. The information is correct but inert.
- It reads like a textbook definition — appropriate for a reference document,
  not for narrative non-fiction aimed at changing how readers understand
  themselves.

---

## DO NOT: Clichéd Analogies (From the Banned List)

> *The unconscious mind is like the part of the iceberg below the waterline.*
> *The brain regions work together like a well-conducted orchestra.*
> *The prefrontal cortex is like the brain's GPS — it navigates us toward
> the right destination.*

**Why these fail:**
- The iceberg: overused to the point of invisibility.
- The orchestra: describes coordination without conveying the actual
  mechanism of how brain regions interact.
- The GPS: implies the prefrontal cortex provides destinations rather
  than management — and is so generic as to describe nothing specifically.
All three are explicitly banned by `system_rules.md` Section 1.3.

# Rule 3: Chapter Hooks

## The Principle

The first paragraph of every chapter must create a reason to keep reading.
This reason cannot be a definition, a statement of chapter contents, or
an abstract claim. It must be one of three things:

1. **A dramatic scene** — Put the reader inside a moment before they know
   what it means. The chapter's thesis is the explanation for the scene.
2. **A bold, surprising claim** — Something that contradicts what the reader
   likely believes, stated with confidence, before it is substantiated.
3. **A precise, compelling question** — One that the reader does not know
   the answer to and genuinely wants answered.

The hook is a contract with the reader: "Stay with me. I am going to pay
this off." The chapter must honour that contract.

---

## DO: Hook by Dramatic Scene

> *She does not know why she is doing this. That is the most important thing
> to understand. Lieutenant Marcia Webb has been fighting fires for eleven
> years. She has a degree in fire science. She can tell you, with precision,
> the flashover temperature of every common construction material and the
> variables that affect the spread rate of a structural fire. She is not a
> person who acts without reason.*
>
> *And yet she is ordering her crew out of a building that every instrument
> and every protocol tells her is safe to be in. She is doing it because
> something — she will not find the word for it until much later — is wrong.
> Not wrong in a way she can measure. Wrong in a way she can feel.*
>
> *What she cannot know, standing in the doorway, is that the floor
> beneath her crew is four inches of timber over a basement full of fire.*

**Why this works:**
- The reader is inside the scene before they know what the chapter is about.
- The dramatic irony (we know, and she doesn't, that she is right) creates
  immediate tension.
- The hook sets up a question the reader desperately wants answered:
  "How did she know?"

---

## DO: Hook by Bold Claim

> *The most important decisions of your professional life were probably not
> made the way you think they were. Not by analysis. Not by deliberation.
> Not by weighing the evidence until the right answer emerged from the data.
> They were made in a moment — a moment preceded by analysis, yes, but
> arriving at its conclusion before the analysis was complete.*
>
> *The research doesn't just suggest this. It demonstrates it, repeatedly,
> across controlled experimental conditions, in populations ranging from
> veteran firefighters to experienced surgeons to financial traders. Expert
> intuition — the kind that turns out to be correct — is not the absence
> of thinking. It is thinking that has been compressed, through years of
> experience, into a signal the body delivers before the mind can catch up.*
>
> *This chapter is the proof.*

**Why this works:**
- The opening is direct and slightly provocative — it challenges the
  reader's self-understanding immediately.
- "This chapter is the proof" is a bold, confident promise. The reader
  knows exactly what they are about to receive.
- The rhythm of the third sentence (a list of three parallel "Not by..."
  constructions) creates cadence before the pivot to what the decisions
  *were* made by.

---

## DO: Hook by Question

> *How much of what you feel instinctively is actually yours?*
>
> *Not learned from your parents, not absorbed from your culture, not
> constructed from the particular sequence of events that make up your
> biography — but genuinely, biologically inherited. Pre-installed.
> Present before you had any experience to learn from.*
>
> *The question seems philosophical. The answer, it turns out, is measured
> in the methylation patterns of your glucocorticoid receptor genes.*

**Why this works:**
- The opening question is genuinely answerable — it's not rhetorical — and
  the reader immediately wants to know the answer.
- The second paragraph expands the question in a way that deepens the
  reader's investment.
- The final sentence pivots from the accessible question to the technical
  precision, creating the anticipation of getting a real, rigorous answer.

---

## DO NOT: Hook by Definition

> *Epigenetics is a field of biology that studies changes in gene expression
> that occur without changes to the underlying DNA sequence. In this chapter,
> we will explore how epigenetic mechanisms may contribute to the inheritance
> of trauma across generations. We will look at case studies including the
> Dutch Hunger Winter and research on Holocaust survivor descendants.*

**Why this fails:**
- The reader has been told what is coming but has not been given a reason
  to care.
- A definition is the correct starting point for an encyclopaedia, not
  for narrative non-fiction.
- The phrase "In this chapter, we will explore" is explicitly banned by
  `system_rules.md` (under "prohibited stylistic patterns" — it signals
  the end of thinking).

---

## DO NOT: Hook by Abstract Claim Without Tension

> *Instinct is a fascinating and complex phenomenon that has been studied
> by scientists and philosophers for centuries. It plays an important role
> in human decision-making and survival. This chapter will examine the
> neuroscience behind instinctive responses and how they influence our
> behaviour in ways we may not be consciously aware of.*

**Why this fails:**
- "Fascinating and complex" are empty modifiers that convey no information.
- "Important role" is vague to the point of meaninglessness.
- The passive construction ("ways we may not be consciously aware of")
  buries the most interesting claim.
- There is no tension, no question, no character, no scene.
  The reader has no reason to turn the page.

# Summary: The Three Rules in Brief

| Rule | Instruction | Test |
|---|---|---|
| **Vary sentence length** | Build with long sentences. Land with short ones. Never three consecutive sentences of the same length. | Read it aloud. Does the rhythm breathe? |
| **Use vivid analogies** | Analogy first. Technical term second. Never abstract-to-abstract. Never from the banned list. | Would the analogy mislead a neuroscientist? If yes, replace it. |
| **Hook every chapter** | Scene, bold claim, or genuine question. Never a definition. Never "In this chapter we will explore." | Does the first paragraph make a contract with the reader? Does the chapter honour it? |
