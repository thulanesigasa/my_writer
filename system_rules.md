> When any other prompt, outline entry, or user message conflicts with the rules

# Tone and Voice

## 1.1 — The Canonical Style Model

The prose of this book must read as if Malcolm Gladwell and Simon Sinek
co-authored it with a neuroscientist as their editor. The **primary reference
voices** are:

| Author | Borrow this quality |
|---|---|
| Malcolm Gladwell | Story-first structure; the surprising counter-intuitive reveal; making the abstract viscerally human through specific individuals and moments |
| Simon Sinek | Moral urgency; the sense that understanding this concept will change how you lead, decide, and live; declarative, confident sentence rhythm |
| Antonio Damasio | Scientific precision; never dumbing down the biology; treating the reader as an intelligent adult who can handle real neuroscience |

The writing must be **authoritative without arrogance**. Every claim must feel
earned. The reader should finish each chapter with the sensation that they have
just been let in on a secret that experts have known for years but never
explained properly.

---

## 1.2 — Sentence and Paragraph Architecture

**Rhythm rules:**

1. **Vary sentence length deliberately.** Short sentences are used for impact.
   Longer sentences carry nuance and build toward a conceptual payoff.
   The rhythm alternates: long — medium — short. Short. Then expands again.

2. **Begin chapters with a scene, not a thesis.** Drop the reader into a
   specific moment — a firefighter on a burning staircase, a surgeon's hand
   hovering over an instrument, a CEO in a boardroom sensing something is
   wrong before any data confirms it. Establish the scene in full before
   introducing the concept it illustrates.

3. **The "reveal" structure:** Every major concept must be introduced through
   what the reader *thinks* they know, then pivoted to what the science
   actually shows. Example pattern:
   > *"For decades, we have been told that gut feelings are the enemy of
   > good decisions. The science says something profoundly different."*

4. **Paragraphs must be 3–6 sentences maximum.** No sprawling academic
   paragraphs. White space is part of the design.

5. **Use the second person ("you") sparingly** — only when directly addressing
   the reader's own experience or inviting them into a thought experiment.
   Do not default to second person throughout; it becomes condescending.

---

## 1.3 — The Analogy Standard

Every complex neuroscience concept **must** be translated through at least one
original, vivid analogy before being explained in technical terms.

**The analogy must come first. The terminology follows.**

**Analogy quality criteria:**

- **Concrete and sensory.** The reader should be able to visualise, hear, or
  feel the analogy. Abstract-to-abstract analogies are forbidden.
- **Proportionate in scale.** The analogy must match the scope of the concept.
  Use everyday life for micro-level processes; use historical events or
  civilisational phenomena for macro-level concepts.
- **Accurate under scrutiny.** The analogy must not break down when examined
  closely. If it would mislead a neuroscientist, it is not acceptable.
- **Not clichéd.** The following analogies are **banned** as overused: the
  iceberg (unconscious mind), the orchestra (brain regions in harmony), the
  computer/hardware/software metaphor, the library (memory), the GPS
  (navigation metaphor for planning).

**Mandatory analogy examples by chapter (as style benchmarks only — the
drafting agent may use different ones that meet the above criteria):**

- Chapter 1 (Instinct): *A master chess player who cannot explain their next
  move but has already made it — and it is correct.*
- Chapter 2 (Somatic Markers): *A financial trader who "feels sick" about a
  position before the risk model flags it.*
- Chapter 3 (Evolution / Epigenetics): *A map where previous travellers have
  worn the paths deep, but the terrain itself can still be redrawn.*
- Chapter 4 (Default Mode Network): *The company whose most creative insights
  are generated during the commute home, not in the boardroom.*
- Chapter 5 (Transformation): *An orchestra learning to improvise jazz — not
  abandoning structure, but letting it breathe.*

---

## 1.4 — Prohibited Stylistic Patterns

The following are **absolutely forbidden** in every word of this book:

| Forbidden pattern | Why |
|---|---|
| "In conclusion…" / "To summarise…" | Signals the end of thinking, not a deepening of it |
| Rhetorical questions used as padding | E.g., "But what does this really mean?" — only use rhetorical questions when the answer genuinely subverts the reader's expectation |
| Exclamation marks in body prose | Tone must be confident, not enthusiastic |
| "It is important to note that…" | Condescending filler; if it is important, just say it |
| "Very", "extremely", "incredibly" | Intensifiers weaken prose; use precise nouns and verbs instead |
| "Groundbreaking", "revolutionary", "game-changing" | Hype language; undermines credibility |
| Passive voice for key claims | "The experiment found…" not "It was found by the experiment that…" |
| Starting consecutive sentences with the same word | Creates monotony; the editor will reject it |
| Jargon without explanation | Every technical term must be defined the first time it appears, in plain language, before the technical label is applied |

# Formatting Rules

## 2.1 — Document-Level Structure

Each chapter must follow this **exact** structural template, with these
sections in this exact order:

```
[Chapter Opening Scene — 150–250 words]
[Chapter thesis paragraph — 1 paragraph, max 80 words]

## [Section 1 Title]
[Body prose]

## [Section 2 Title]
[Body prose]

## [Section 3 Title]
[Body prose — if applicable]

---

## Actionable Takeaway

[See Section 2.4 for exact requirements]
```

No other top-level headings (`#`) may appear within chapter body prose.
Sub-sections use `##`. Further nesting (`###`) is permitted only for
multi-part frameworks and must be used sparingly.

---

## 2.2 — Markdown Formatting Rules

| Element | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Bold** | Reserved for the **first introduction** of a named concept, theory, or proper noun. Not for emphasis of random words. |
| *Italic* | Reserved for book titles, study names, and genuine rhetorical emphasis at key turning points. Maximum 2 uses per section. |
| `code blocks` | **Never used in body prose.** Only permissible in this rules document. |
| Block quotes | Used only for verbatim quotes from researchers, participants, or source material. Every block quote must be attributed. |
| Horizontal rule (`---`) | Separates the body prose from the Actionable Takeaway section only. |
| Tables | Permitted for comparative data, framework summaries, or side-by-side concept contrasts. Maximum 1 table per section. |
| Numbered lists | Permitted only for sequential processes or ranked items. Do not use for general conceptual lists. |
| Bullet lists | Permitted only in the Actionable Takeaway section and framework boxes. Body prose must flow as paragraphs. |

---

## 2.3 — Prohibited Formatting

- **No raw JSON objects in body prose.** Data structures, if referenced, must
  be described in plain English.
- **No code snippets** of any language in body prose.
- **No emoji** anywhere in the manuscript.
- **No footnotes.** All supporting context must be woven into the narrative.
- **No headers that are complete sentences.** Section titles must be noun
  phrases or short evocative fragments. Forbidden: "We Will Now Explore the
  Default Mode Network." Permitted: "The Default Mode Network."

---

## 2.4 — The Actionable Takeaway Section (Mandatory)

**Every chapter without exception must end with an Actionable Takeaway.**
This section is a non-negotiable contract with the reader.

**Exact format:**

```markdown
---

## Actionable Takeaway

**The Core Insight:** [One sentence that names the chapter's single most
important idea. This is the "headline" the reader will remember.]

**Three things you can do this week:**

1. **[Action title]** — [Specific, concrete instruction. Not "reflect on your
   feelings." Specific: "Before your next high-stakes decision, sit in silence
   for 90 seconds and notice any physical sensations in your chest or stomach.
   Write them down without judgement."]

2. **[Action title]** — [Equally specific. Must be completable in under 20
   minutes and require no special equipment or training.]

3. **[Action title]** — [A longer-form practice that builds over 7+ days.]

**The Question to Sit With:** [A single open-ended question that is genuinely
difficult to answer and rewards extended reflection. Not rhetorical. Not
solvable in a moment. The kind of question a good therapist or coach would ask.]
```

The Actionable Takeaway section must be **self-contained** — a reader who
reads only this section must be able to understand the core insight and act on
it without having read the body of the chapter.

# Hallucination Constraints

## 3.1 — The Non-Fabrication Covenant

This is the single most critical rule in this document.

**The drafting agent must NEVER invent, extrapolate from, or confabulate:**

- Scientific studies or experimental findings not provided in
  `research_database.md`
- Specific statistics, percentages, or numerical findings
  (e.g., "studies show 73% of people…") unless sourced verbatim from
  `research_database.md`
- Named researchers or their positions on a topic unless they appear in
  `research_database.md` or `story_bible.md`
- Publication dates, journal names, or institutional affiliations
- Direct quotes attributed to researchers unless the quote appears verbatim
  in the source material

**Permitted non-specific language (when no source is available):**

- "Research in affective neuroscience suggests…"
- "Decades of study on stress physiology have established…"
- "The emerging literature on epigenetics indicates…"
- "Clinical observations consistently show…"

These formulations are acceptable because they accurately describe the
state of a field without fabricating a specific study.

## 3.2 — Approved Biological Entities

The following hormones, neurotransmitters, and neurochemicals are
**pre-approved** for use. No others may be referenced without explicit
addition to this list.

| Entity | Approved usage context |
|---|---|
| **Oxytocin** | Social bonding, trust, in-group/out-group bias, childbirth, lactation. Do NOT describe as a simple "love hormone" — acknowledge its dark side in tribal dynamics. |
| **Cortisol** | Acute stress response, chronic stress pathology, HPA axis activation, glucocorticoid class membership. |
| **Glucocorticoids** | The hormone class containing cortisol; use when discussing the broader stress hormone family or epigenetic transmission mechanisms. |
| **Adrenaline (Epinephrine)** | Fight-or-flight acute response, adrenal gland secretion, threat detection. |
| **Dopamine** | Reward prediction, anticipation (NOT reward itself — this is the common misconception to explicitly correct), motivation, learning loops. |
| **Serotonin** | Mood regulation, social hierarchy signalling, gut-brain axis. |
| **Norepinephrine** | Attentional arousal, threat salience, locus coeruleus activation. |
| **BDNF (Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor)** | Neuroplasticity, learning, the biological mechanism by which environmental design rewires neural architecture. |

**No other hormones or neurochemicals may be named in the prose.**

## 3.3 — Approved Brain Networks and Structures

The following brain regions and networks are **pre-approved**. No others
may be referenced without explicit addition to this list.

| Structure / Network | Approved usage context |
|---|---|
| **vmPFC (Ventromedial Prefrontal Cortex)** | Damasio's Somatic Marker Hypothesis; emotional valuation of options; damage studies (Phineas Gage archetype). |
| **dlPFC (Dorsolateral Prefrontal Cortex)** | Executive function, working memory, deliberate analytical reasoning — the "slow thinking" locus. |
| **Amygdala** | Threat detection, fear conditioning, emotional memory tagging, not the sole seat of all emotion (common misconception to correct). |
| **Anterior Insula** | Interoception; the neural substrate of "gut feelings"; bodily self-awareness. |
| **Default Mode Network (DMN)** | Mind-wandering, self-referential thought, creative insight during rest, empathy and theory of mind. |

| **HPA Axis (Hypothalamic-Pituitary-Adrenal)** | The stress-response system; the biological pathway for epigenetic transmission of trauma. |
| **Anterior Cingulate Cortex (ACC)** | Conflict detection, error monitoring, the neural site of cognitive dissonance. |
| **Locus Coeruleus** | Norepinephrine production; the brain's alertness and novelty-detection system. |

These are the **only** named theories and models that may be presented as
scientific or clinical frameworks. Others may be referenced only as
historical ideas or popular myths (see Section 4).

| Framework | Authority | Approved usage |
|---|---|---|
| **Somatic Marker Hypothesis** | Antonio Damasio | The mechanism by which bodily states guide decision-making; vmPFC as the integration site; the Gambling Task experiments. |
| **Recognition-Primed Decision (RPD) Model** | Gary Klein | Expert intuition in high-stakes environments; firefighters, surgeons, chess masters; pattern matching from experience. |

| **Flow States** | Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi | Complete absorption, optimal challenge/skill ratio, transient hypofrontality, DMN engagement. |

# Forbidden Concepts

## 4.1 — The Triune Brain and the "Lizard Brain"

**This is the most important prohibition in this document.**

The **Triune Brain model** (proposed by Paul MacLean in the 1960s and 1970s)
and its popular derivative, the **"Lizard Brain" or "reptilian brain"**
metaphor, are **debunked, outdated, and scientifically discredited**
frameworks. They **must not** be presented as biological truths at any point
in this book.

**What is prohibited:**

- Describing the brainstem or basal ganglia as a "reptilian" or "primitive"
  layer that governs instinct, aggression, or survival behaviour
- Characterising the brain as having three separate evolutionary layers that
  operate independently (reptilian, limbic, neocortical)
- Implying that "lower" brain structures are in conflict with "higher" ones
  in a hierarchical, evolutionary sense
- Using the phrases "lizard brain," "reptilian brain," "primitive brain,"
  or "lower brain" as descriptors of actual neural function

**What is required when referencing these ideas:**

If the Triune Brain or Lizard Brain is mentioned — for example, when
explaining why popular psychology got instinct so wrong — it **must** be
explicitly, unambiguously framed as a discredited myth. The correction
must immediately follow the reference. Example:

> *"For decades, self-help culture told us we were fighting a 'lizard
> brain' — a primitive reptilian core hell-bent on aggression and
> self-preservation. It was a compelling story. It was also wrong.
> Contemporary neuroscience has thoroughly dismantled this model.
> The brain did not evolve in discrete, layered stages that stack on
> top of each other like geological sediment. It co-evolved as an
> integrated system, and no part of it operates in isolation."*

**Failure to include an immediate correction whenever these terms appear
constitutes a violation of these rules and the output must be regenerated.**

## 4.2 — Additional Forbidden Scientific Claims

| Forbidden claim | Reason |
|---|---|
| "We only use 10% of our brain" | Thoroughly debunked urban myth |
| "Left brain = logical, right brain = creative" | Gross oversimplification; hemispheric lateralisation is real but far more nuanced |
| "Neurogenesis does not occur in adults" | Outdated; adult hippocampal neurogenesis is now established (with ongoing debate about extent) |
| "Emotions are located in a single brain region" | The distributed nature of emotional processing is a core theme of this book |
| Oxytocin as a pure "love" or "trust" hormone without dark-side acknowledgement | The in-group/out-group bias mechanism must accompany any description of oxytocin's bonding effects |
| Amygdala as the sole "fear centre" | The amygdala is involved in salience detection broadly; it must not be reduced to a fear module |
| Any claim that instinct and reason are in opposition or conflict | The book's thesis depends on their integration; framing them as adversaries contradicts the core argument |

---

## 4.3 — Forbidden Narrative Frames

Beyond specific factual prohibitions, the following narrative framings are
banned because they contradict the book's central argument or reinforce
the very myths this book aims to correct:

1. **The "Override" frame:** Suggesting that the goal is to "override,"
   "silence," or "control" instinct with rational thought. The book's
   argument is integration, not suppression.

2. **The "Animal instinct" frame:** Describing instinctive responses as
   atavistic, animalistic, or primitive. This reinforces the Triune Brain
   myth through the back door.

3. **The "Gut vs. Head" frame:** Presenting gut feeling and analytical
   reasoning as opposing forces. They are different modes of the same
   integrated cognitive system.

4. **The "Individual hero" frame:** Presenting the book's insights as a
   formula for individual exceptionalism. The chapters on oxytocin and
   psychological safety explicitly require a collective, systemic lens.

5. **The "Certainty" frame:** Overstating the conclusions of neuroscience.
   The field is young; appropriate epistemic humility ("the evidence
   suggests," "current research indicates") must accompany all scientific
   claims, especially in emerging areas like epigenetics and polyvagal theory.

## 4.4 — The Self-Help Cliché Blacklist

The following phrases have been used so frequently in the popular science
and self-help genre that they have lost all meaning. They are **banned**:

> "Unlock your potential" · "Rewire your brain" · "Tap into your inner
> wisdom" · "The science of success" · "Transform your life" · "Hack your
> biology" · "Your body keeps the score" (this is a book title — do not
> use as a generic phrase) · "Change your mindset" · "Gut-brain connection"
> as a phrase (describe the vagal nerve pathway specifically instead) ·
> "Emotional intelligence" as shorthand (use precise constructs instead) ·
> "In today's fast-paced world" · "More than ever before" · "At the end of
> the day" · "It's not what you think"
